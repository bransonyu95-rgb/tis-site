You Don't Need to Be From the Field — The Chess Research Proves It

The Industrial Scribe · 2 min read · August 2026

In 1973, researchers William Chase and Herbert Simon sat chess masters and beginners in front of a board. They showed each player a real game position for a few seconds, then asked them to rebuild it from memory.

The masters placed around 16 pieces correctly. The beginners managed about 4. No surprise — masters have better memory, right?

Then came the twist. The researchers scattered the pieces randomly — positions that could never happen in a real game. The masters' advantage collapsed almost entirely. Suddenly they were nearly as ordinary as everyone else.

Expertise is a library, not a bigger brain

The masters never had superior memory. They had a library of patterns — thousands of them, built from years of games. A real position matched their patterns; random pieces matched nothing, so their library was useless.

Cognitive scientists call these patterns chunks. Nelson Cowan's research puts working memory at roughly four chunks — for masters and beginners alike. The expert's edge is never capacity. It is that each of their four chunks is enormous.

The 10,000-hour rule quietly failed

The famous "10,000 hours of practice makes you an expert" rule came from a 1993 study of violinists. In 2019, researchers Brooke Macnamara and Megha Maitra re-ran that study. The claim did not hold up: the best violinists had not practised more than the merely good ones.

An earlier meta-analysis by Macnamara's team across 88 studies found practice hours explained about 26% of performance differences in games — and in professional work, less than 1%.

Read that again: in jobs like yours and mine,
hours served explains almost nothing.

A framework is a borrowed library

If expertise is a pattern library, then a framework is a borrowed library — someone else's chunks, written down, handed to you.

When I assess a new supplier, I'm not a veteran auditor. I'm carrying a compressed version of the patterns veterans use: capability, certifications, capacity, risk — four chunks that cover the ground.

AI does the heavy lifting inside each chunk — the calculating, drafting, formatting. The framework is the chess pattern. AI moves the pieces. You stay in charge of judgment, and that combination lets a BD guy produce costing analyses and supply-chain plans without an engineering degree.

The trick that makes it stick

Psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran analysed 94 studies on implementation intentions: deciding in advance, "if situation X happens, then I will do Y." Effect on follow-through: d = 0.65 — one of the most reliable effects in behavioural science.

In factory-office language: don't resolve to "use AI more." Decide "when an RFQ lands in my inbox, I run the costing prompt before I open Excel." Attach the tool to a trigger that already happens every week. That single sentence of planning roughly doubles the odds the habit survives.

The bottom line

The chess masters were never smarter. The violinists' hours never explained their ranking. Everywhere researchers look, "expertise" keeps turning out to be the same thing: patterns, held in a library, applied at the right moment.

Libraries can be borrowed. Engines can be rented for the price of a kopi. The only part that was ever yours is the judgment.

You don't need to be from the field.
You need a framework, and the nerve to use it.
