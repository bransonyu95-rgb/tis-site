The Email I Never Sent

The Industrial Scribe · 2 min read · August 2026

There is a version of me, years long, who knew the bad news and said nothing.

Not once. A pattern. The delivery slips, I know it on Monday, and the email sits half-written while I tell myself professional-sounding lies: waiting for confirmation. Better to have the full picture. Maybe production can still catch up.

The truth is simpler. I was afraid. Afraid of the customer's reply. Afraid of management seeing the mess with my name on it.

So customers found out the worst way there is: from the calendar. Some noticed before the date. Some only after it had quietly passed. Every time, the same result — disappointment from the customer, disappointment from management, and a small permanent dent in the thing a BD person lives on: reputation.

Nobody shouted much. They just trusted me a little less. That's the real price of silence — it's paid in instalments.

The turn wasn't a moment

It was stranger than that. I started talking to AI — not to write my emails, at first, but to think. Kept digging: what's actually inside this? What am I telling myself when I don't press send?

And the fear finally had a shape I could look at: I wasn't protecting the customer from bad news. I was protecting myself from their reaction. Once I could see that, I could face it accordingly.

Rules instead of feelings

Now I run on three rules. Bad news beats no news, always. The objective is not a happy customer — it's a customer who has nothing left to demand, because the one line of bad news comes with the whole recovery plan attached: what's done, what's next, the new date, and when I'll update them again even if nothing changes.

And here's the honest part, because this site doesn't do fake transformations: the fear is still there. Even now. I just tell myself what I'm telling you — and send anyway. It feels better than the silence ever did. It keeps me going.

The email goes out the same day now.
That's the whole difference.

The thinking behind it

Naming the fear is not just talk

What happened in those AI conversations has a name in psychology: affect labeling. Matthew Lieberman's lab found that putting a feeling into words — even one word — measurably reduces activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm system. And Ethan Kross's research shows that examining your own situation from a distance, the way you'd analyse someone else's problem, produces insight where staring at it from inside produces only more stress.

That's what "keep on asking what's inside" actually does. You're not being soft. You're switching off the alarm long enough to act.
