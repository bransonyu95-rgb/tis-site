The idea

There is no standard method in manufacturing. The right way to price a job, structure a report, or negotiate depends on the nature of your business. Most people skip this diagnosis and copy whatever template they found first. That is why templates fail.

Your business nature is not what you make — it is how the market lets you make money. Two shops with identical machines can be opposite businesses. Every bad commercial decision I have seen up close is a nature mismatch: acting like the business you wish you were, instead of the one you are.

The three natures + one overlay, in full

Nature A — Commodity Volume: the market sets your price
  • How money is really made: utilisation. Machines running = money; machines waiting = losses. Margin comes from efficiency, never from price.
  • What customers actually buy: the absence of problems. Nobody remembers a good delivery; everyone remembers a late one.
  • Warning signs: you win only when you are cheapest; the annual cost-down eats your whole productivity gain.
  • Classic mistake: buying volume with below-floor pricing that never recovers.
  • Growth path: scale up — or climb toward B or C with the profits. Standing still means shrinking margins forever.
Nature B — High-Mix Custom: speed sets your price
  • How money is really made: response time. The first credible answer to an enquiry wins much of the time, almost regardless of price. Your real product is certainty, delivered fast.
  • What customers actually buy: a solved problem. They arrive with a drawing and a deadline.
  • Warning signs: enquiries pile up unanswered; one senior estimator is the bottleneck; you lose jobs you never got to answer.
  • Classic mistake: pricing custom work like commodity work — charging for hours instead of capability.
  • Growth path: systematise the knowledge in your senior people's heads (this is where AI is almost unfair), then qualify into C-sectors.
Nature C — Qualified Precision: the moat sets your price
  • How money is really made: the qualification, not the parts. Your customer spent 6–18 months auditing you; replacing you costs the same again. That switching cost IS your margin.
  • What customers actually buy: risk elimination. They pay premium so nothing ever surprises them.
  • Warning signs: you price against unqualified competitors (they cannot even bid); you treat audit prep as a burden instead of as the product.
  • Classic mistake: giving away the premium your qualification earns.
  • Growth path: more qualifications, deeper into the same customers' programs, longer agreements. Being impossible to remove beats being big.
The Overlay — Captive / Tier-Dependent: the relationship sets your price

Not a fourth box — a condition on top of A, B, or C. A captive commodity shop and a captive precision shop face completely different risks. Captivity changes your risk, not your craft.

  • How money is really made: embeddedness — their process assumes you exist.
  • Warning signs: you cannot name your #3 customer; price cuts arrive as announcements, not negotiations.
  • The rule: serve the big customer excellently, measure your value contribution in RM every quarter, and treat building customer #3 as insurance you pay for monthly.
