How to sell AI automation services to small factories

By Ah Hock · 11 November 2026

Short answer: Sell one finished document, not "AI". Offer a fixed-price two-week pilot that takes one person's most-hated weekly task, like the Monday production report, and measure the time before and after. Get written permission before any factory file touches your computer, and make sure a human approves every draft before it goes anywhere.

I work in the office of a factory in the semiconductor industry, in module assembly. I am not a consultant. I am the person you would be pitching to. So this is the buyer's side of the table: what would make me say yes, and what would make me show you the door.

Who actually buys this in a small factory?

Two people, and you need both.

The one who feels the pain is in the office: the planner who builds the weekly report, the QA engineer who writes the customer complaint replies, the purchasing person chasing late material. They will tell you exactly which task eats their evening.

The one who signs is the owner or the general manager. They do not care about tools. They care whether the report still arrives on Monday, whether the customer stops sending replies back, and whether anything leaves the building that should not.

Find the first person, prove it on their task, and let them carry the result to the second.

What does a factory office actually pay for?

A named task with a before and after. Nobody in my office has ever asked to buy "automation". Everybody would like their evening back.

The tasks worth naming are the boring ones that repeat:

  • The weekly production report, built every Monday from an ERP export
  • SOPs that need writing before the next audit
  • 8D replies to customer complaints, which customers send back when root cause is unclear
  • The hard emails: late delivery, price change, chasing a supplier

Pick one. Say it in the factory's words. "Your Monday report, drafted from your own export, ready for you to check" is a sentence a GM understands in one read.

How should I start: what does a pilot look like?

Small, fixed-price, and measured. A simple ladder:

  1. Discovery visit. Sit with one office person, find their most-hated weekly task, and show it being done live on sample data. This is your sales call.
  2. Pilot. Set up one workflow for one person over two weeks. Write down how long the task took before and how long it takes after. That sheet is your receipt.
  3. Full setup. Two or three workflows, plus training the staff to check and approve drafts.
  4. Monthly care. A small retainer: check in, improve what drifted, add one workflow a month.

Run the pilot side by side with the old way until the office trusts it. Do not switch off the manual report on day one.

How much should I charge?

This is my opinion from the Malaysian SME world in 2026, not industry data. Adjust it to your market and your proof:

StepPrice range (my opinion)
Discovery visitFree to RM 500
Two-week pilotRM 1,500 to 5,000
Full setup and trainingRM 3,000 to 10,000
Monthly careRM 300 to 1,500 a month

Start at the low end until you have two before-and-after sheets from real pilots. Raise prices with proof. Adjectives will not do it.

Also tell the client the running cost up front. If they keep the setup, they pay for their own AI account. A paste-box tool runs on a free ChatGPT or Claude plan; the Claude Code version needs Claude Pro at USD 20 a month. A factory owner who finds a surprise bill in month two will not renew your care contract.

What will the factory worry about, and how do I answer?

"Our data leaves the building." Get a signed permission letter before any file enters your folder. Keep one folder per client. Delete on request. If the files hold personal data about individuals, Malaysia's PDPA applies to you, so mask names when in doubt. Customer drawings and part numbers are often under NDA too, so ask before you paste them anywhere.

"Will it send things by itself?" It should not, and you should say so in the proposal. My rule is that agents draft and a person decides. Nothing sends, posts or publishes on its own. From where I sit, that sentence matters more to a boss than the demo does.

"What if it gets the numbers wrong?" It will, sometimes. That is why a person checks every draft, and why the pilot runs next to the manual version first. Say this before they ask.

"Can't we just do it ourselves?" Often, yes. Tell them so. What they pay you for is that they do not have the time, you have done it before, and you will still be there next month. In my experience on the buying side, that honest answer builds more trust than dodging the question.

Can I use a ready-made tool for client work?

Yes, if its terms allow it. Check that first. Mine do. The tool I would start a pilot with is The Waste Hunt (USD 3.99 once). It is a 30-minute AI interview on one task the client redoes every week: it maps where the hours go using the lean 8 wastes, checks the files behind the task, and tells you what to cut, what to automate and how far. Run it in the discovery call and you walk out with the pilot scope written in the client's own words. It runs in any AI chat, free plans included, or as a Claude Code skill. See The Waste Hunt and its honest limits.

The terms allow two ways of serving a client:

  • You run it for them. The tool stays on your computer and you deliver the finished documents. You may charge for that service, with as many clients as you like.
  • You set it up at their place. One license means one business using it. A client who wants their own copy buys their own, priced like lunch, and you charge for your setup work.

You may not resell or share the files themselves.

What should I never promise?

Never promise what the demo did not show. Do not quote time savings you have not measured on their task, and do not promise it fixes a problem that is not a writing problem. A late supplier is still late after the email is well written.

Under-promise, run the pilot, and hand over the before-and-after sheet. In a small factory, the owner talks to other owners. One sheet with real minutes on it will sell your next pilot better than anything you say.
